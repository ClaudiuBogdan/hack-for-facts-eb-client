# Your Budget in Context

## Totals matter, but they are not enough

The first thing you see is the total: how much money came in and how much was spent. That is a necessary reference point, but it is not yet a conclusion.

A big total can simply mean a big locality. A smaller total can simply mean a smaller one. To understand scale better, you need context.

## Per capita changes the reading

When you divide the total by population, you get a per-capita value. That helps you see how much money corresponds, on average, to each inhabitant and makes comparisons much more useful.

Per capita does not tell you everything about service quality, but it helps you avoid comparing very different places only through raw totals.

## What changed from 2024 to 2025

Then look at the change between 2024 and 2025. The useful question is not only "did it go up or down?" but also "by how much?".

This is where two practical tools matter:

- year-over-year comparison
- inflation adjustment

Without inflation adjustment, older years can look smaller than they really are in purchasing-power terms. With inflation adjustment on, earlier years are recalculated in constant 2024 prices.

<ExpandableHint label="How to read inflation and per-capita correctly">
- Per-capita mode uses the same reference population across the displayed years to keep comparisons consistent.
- Inflation adjustment recalculates earlier years in 2024 prices. This helps you compare real purchasing power more fairly.
- In this module we keep peer comparison light. The goal here is to understand your city hall better, not to do a full comparative study yet.
</ExpandableHint>

## County context

A budget does not say much in isolation. When you compare your city hall's per-capita spending or income with other localities in the same county, you start to see where it stands. A higher per-capita value does not automatically mean waste, and a lower one does not mean efficiency. But large differences raise questions worth investigating.
