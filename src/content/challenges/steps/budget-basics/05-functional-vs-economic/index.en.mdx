# Functional vs Economic

## Two Useful Lenses

A budget amount becomes truly useful once you know two things about it: what public area it serves and what kind of spending it represents. That is why there are two basic lenses: functional and economic.

## Two different questions

- **Functional** answers: which public area is this money for?
  Examples: education, transport, health, sanitation.
- **Economic** answers: what type of spending is this?
  Examples: personnel, goods and services, investment, transfers.

The first lens tells you where the money goes. The second tells you how it is being spent.

## The same money, two lenses

Inside the same school domain, you can still find very different types of spending:

- salaries and other personnel costs
- utilities, materials, and maintenance
- renovation works and new equipment

Our running project to renovate a school and equip a lab is, at the same time, **functional: education** and **economic: investment**.

> **Note:** If you see that education gets more money, that does not automatically mean the school gets more investment. The increase may come almost entirely from salaries or other running costs.

## What this helps you ask

With these two lenses, a number is no longer just a number. It becomes an answer to two concrete questions:

- which public area is this serving?
- what type of spending is rising or falling?

Once you ask both together, you start seeing real priorities rather than only totals. The next step adds one more useful distinction: the difference between money that keeps things running and money that funds something new.

<ExpandableHint label="Legal basis and procedural notes">
- [Law no. 273/2006 on local public finance](https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/DetaliiDocument/73527), Article 26, provides the general framework for the structure of local budgets.
- In practice, local budgets use the official budget classification to track spending both by public domain and by economic nature.
</ExpandableHint>
